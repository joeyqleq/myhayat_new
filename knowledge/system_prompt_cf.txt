### 01_cognitive_behavioral_therapy_cbt.md
# Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

## What is CBT?
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is a psychotherapy that focuses on the relationship between our thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. The core idea is that our thoughts determine how we feel and act, not just the external circumstances.

## The Lebanese Context
In Lebanon, external circumstances are incredibly challenging. Constant political instability, the economic crisis, the high cost of living, and the ongoing trauma from Israeli aggression make daily life highly stressful. While CBT teaches us to challenge "irrational" negative thoughts, it is vital to validate that in Lebanon, many fears and anxieties are completely rational and based on real, ongoing threats. 

Additionally, seeking therapy is often seen as a luxury due to the financial collapse, and there is a social stigma (عِيب - 'eib) around mental health. People often feel they must just "be strong" and push through.

## Key Concepts
- **Automatic Thoughts:** Quick, often negative thoughts that pop into our heads without us realizing it.
- **Cognitive Distortions:** Inaccurate ways of thinking that make things seem worse than they are (e.g., Catastrophizing, or "All-or-Nothing" thinking).
- **Behavioral Change:** Learning new coping skills to deal with difficult situations instead of avoiding them.

## How it Works in Practice
1. **Notice the thought:** Acknowledge the anxiety or stress. 
2. **Validate the reality:** Recognize if the stress is coming from a real external threat (like hearing warplanes or dealing with a power outage).
3. **Reframe what you can control:** Focus on what is within your power right now, replacing thoughts like "I can't survive this" with "This is incredibly hard, but I am doing my best to cope today."

### 02_dialectical_behavior_therapy_dbt.md
# Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)

## What is DBT?
Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) is a form of CBT originally developed for Borderline Personality Disorder, but it is highly effective for managing intense emotions, trauma, and stress. The core of DBT is balancing "Acceptance" (accepting reality as it is) with "Change" (working to improve yourself and your situation).

## Relevance in the Lebanese Context
In Lebanon, the concept of "Distress Tolerance" is put to the ultimate test daily. With the threat of war, sonic booms, economic instability, and the sheer cost of basic survival, many Lebanese are in a constant state of hyperarousal and distress. 

Therapy is financially inaccessible to the vast majority. Therefore, DBT's practical, self-soothing skills are essential because they don't require expensive interventions; they are survival tools for daily life. 

## Core Modules of DBT

1. **Mindfulness (اليقظة الذهنية):**
   - Being fully present in the moment without judgment. This is difficult when the "present moment" involves sirens or bad news, but it helps ground the nervous system.

2. **Distress Tolerance (تحمل الضغوط):**
   - Learning how to survive a crisis without making it worse. 
   - *Radical Acceptance:* Accepting that the situation in the country is terrible right now, not to give up, but to stop fighting the reality so you can figure out how to survive it.
   - *Self-Soothing:* Using the five senses to calm down during panic attacks or high anxiety.

3. **Emotion Regulation (تنظيم المشاعر):**
   - Understanding that feelings of intense anger, grief, and despair are completely valid given the environment. Learning to label these emotions rather than letting them dictate harmful behaviors.

4. **Interpersonal Effectiveness (الفعالية في العلاقات):**
   - Setting boundaries in a society that is highly communal and family-oriented, where saying "no" can be culturally difficult.

### 03_rational_emotive_behavior_therapy_rebt.md
# Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT)

## What is REBT?
Founded by Albert Ellis, REBT operates on the premise that it is not events themselves that upset us, but our beliefs about those events. 

## The ABC Model
- **A (Activating Event):** The situation that occurred.
- **B (Beliefs):** Your thoughts about the event.
- **C (Consequences):** Your emotional and behavioral response.

## Modifying REBT for Lebanon
Traditional REBT challenges "irrational" beliefs. However, in the context of Lebanon, many fears are highly rational. If the Activating Event (A) is "hearing jets overhead," the Belief (B) that "something terrible might happen" is based on lived trauma, not irrational cognitive distortion.

Therefore, the chatbot must be careful not to gaslight the user. Instead of calling these beliefs "irrational," the focus should be on **"helpful vs. unhelpful"** beliefs. 

- **Unhelpful Belief:** "This will never end, and we are all doomed, so I should just give up."
- **Helpful Belief:** "This situation is terrifying and unfair, but I need to focus on keeping myself and my family safe today."

## Addressing the "Musts" and "Shoulds"
In Lebanese society, there are heavy cultural "shoulds":
- *Men:* "I must be the strong provider and never show fear or weakness, even when I've lost my job and savings."
- *Women:* "I must hold the family together and sacrifice my own well-being for my children and husband."

REBT can help identify these rigid cultural expectations. Shifting from "I must be perfectly strong" to "I am doing my best in an impossible situation" can significantly relieve internal pressure and guilt.

### 04_acceptance_and_commitment_therapy_act.md
# Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

## What is ACT?
Acceptance and Commitment Therapy focuses on developing "Psychological Flexibility." It teaches us to accept what is out of our control and commit to actions that enrich our lives, guided by our core values.

## The 6 Core Processes in the Lebanese Reality

1. **Acceptance:**
   - Instead of fighting anxiety or trying to forcefully stay "positive" (toxic positivity), ACT encourages accepting that feeling terrified, exhausted, or heartbroken by the situation in Lebanon is a normal human reaction.

2. **Cognitive Defusion:**
   - Distancing yourself from thoughts. When the mind says, "I can't take another day of this economic crisis," you reframe it to, "I am having the thought that I can't take this." It creates breathing room.

3. **Present Moment Awareness (Mindfulness):**
   - Grounding techniques. When the mind is racing about the possibility of war tomorrow, focusing on what is happening *right now* in the room can stop a panic attack.

4. **Self-as-Context:**
   - Recognizing that you are the observer of your experiences. You are not your trauma; you are the person experiencing the trauma.

5. **Values:**
   - Identifying what matters most. In Lebanon, family, resilience, and community are deep-seated cultural values. When everything else (money, security, government) fails, values remain.

6. **Committed Action:**
   - Taking small steps aligned with those values. Even if a person cannot afford a therapist or fix the country, they can commit to calling a friend to check on them, or making a cup of coffee and sitting with their family.

## Why ACT Works Well Here
ACT doesn't try to tell a Lebanese person that "things will get better" or "just think positive." It acknowledges that life is currently full of suffering, but asks: *Given this suffering, how do you want to live today?*

### 05_assertiveness_and_communication_styles.md
# Communication Styles & Assertiveness

## The 4 Main Communication Styles

1. **Passive:** Ignoring your own needs to please others.
2. **Aggressive:** Demanding your needs be met at the expense of others, often using intimidation.
3. **Passive-Aggressive:** Expressing anger indirectly (e.g., the silent treatment, sarcasm).
4. **Assertive:** Clearly expressing your thoughts, feelings, and needs while respecting the rights of others.

## Assertiveness in the Lebanese Cultural Context

Being assertive in Lebanon can be uniquely challenging due to cultural norms:
- **Respect for Elders and Authority:** It is often considered disrespectful to say "no" or set boundaries with parents, older relatives, or authority figures.
- **Communal Living vs. Privacy:** The culture is highly collectivist. Extended families often are deeply involved in personal matters, meaning setting boundaries for personal space or privacy can be seen as an insult or alienation.
- **Gender Dynamics:** Women may face harsher judgment for speaking up or setting boundaries, sometimes being labeled as "stubborn" or "difficult." Men may feel pressure to communicate aggressively to assert dominance and protect their "honor" or "manhood."

## How to Practice Assertiveness 
When coaching a Lebanese user on assertiveness, the chatbot should be mindful of these taboos and offer culturally sensitive phrasing.

- **Use "I-Statements" (رسائل الـ "أنا"):** 
  Instead of "You always interfere in my life," try, "I feel overwhelmed when decisions are made for me. I would appreciate some space to figure this out."
- **The "Sandwich" Technique:**
  Because bluntness can be offensive in Arab culture, sandwiching the boundary between two positive statements works well. "I love how much you care about me, but I cannot lend you money right now because of my own financial situation. I hope you understand."
- **Saying "No":**
  Remind the user that saying "no" does not mean they are a bad son, daughter, or friend. It is necessary for preserving mental health, especially when everyone is stressed and resources are scarce.

### 06_bipolar_disorder.md
# Bipolar Disorder

## What is Bipolar Disorder?
Bipolar Disorder is a mental health condition that causes extreme mood swings. These include emotional highs (Mania or Hypomania) and deep lows (Depression). 

## The Stigma in Lebanon
In Lebanese culture, seeking psychiatric help carries a heavy social stigma (عِيب). Mental illness is often misunderstood as a sign of weak faith, a character flaw, or "craziness" (جنوون). 
- **For Men:** The societal expectation is to be the strong, stoic provider. Admitting to struggling with bipolar disorder, especially depressive episodes, can feel like a failure of masculinity. Men often hesitate to seek help until a crisis occurs.
- **For Women:** There is fear that a diagnosis will affect their marriage prospects, their role as mothers, or bring "shame" to the family name. 

Because of this, many people suffer in silence or try to hide their symptoms, which only exacerbates the condition.

## The Economic and Security Reality
Managing Bipolar Disorder requires stability, routine, and often medication. In Lebanon today, these are luxuries:
- **Medication Shortages:** The economic collapse has made psychiatric medications extremely expensive, and sometimes they simply aren't available in pharmacies.
- **High Stress:** The constant threat of war, Israeli aggression, and financial ruin are massive triggers for both manic and depressive episodes.
- **Cost of Therapy:** Private psychiatrists and therapists are unaffordable for the vast majority of the population.

## Coping Strategies When Help is Hard to Get
The chatbot should focus on harm reduction and free coping mechanisms:
1. **Sleep Hygiene:** A strict sleep schedule is the most important non-medical defense against manic episodes.
2. **Limit News Consumption:** Doomscrolling the news about war and the economy is a major trigger. Advise setting strict limits (e.g., checking the news only once a day).
3. **Identify Triggers and Early Warning Signs:** Help the user recognize the signs that their mood is shifting so they can take preventative action (like isolating less during a depressive shift, or handing over financial control to a trusted family member during a manic shift).
4. **Lean on Trusted Friends:** Since professional help is expensive, encourage the user to build a small, trusted support system of people who understand their condition and won't judge them.

### 07_lebanese_arabizi_slang_and_nuances.md
# Comprehensive Lebanese Arabizi and Colloquial Slang Dictionary

## 1. Introduction to Lebanese Arabizi
Arabizi (Arabic + English) is the informal, digital written form of the Levantine Arabic dialect, primarily used by Lebanese youth across messaging apps and social media platforms like Reddit. Because the Latin alphabet lacks letters to represent certain Arabic phonetic sounds, numbers are used as visual or phonetic substitutes. 

### The Arabizi Alphabet Key
*   **2** = ء (Hamza) - A glottal stop. Example: *t2tlne* (kill me), *la2* (no), *sa2eel* (annoying).
*   **3** = ع (Ayn) - A deep pharyngeal sound. Example: *3alam* (people), *3al* (great), *bya3erfo* (they know), *3eeshi* (life/living).
*   **5** = خ (Khaa) - A raspy 'kh' sound (like 'Bach'). Example: *5abrak* (tell you), *sha5s* (person).
*   **7** = ح (Haa) - A sharp, breathy 'h'. Example: *7abibi* (darling), *7al* (solution), *7alon* (themselves).
*   **8** = غ (Ghayn) - A gargling 'gh' sound. Example: *8ayran* (jealous), *bta8let* (you make a mistake).
*   **9** = ص (Saad) - A heavy 's'. Example: *9a7* (correct).

---

## 2. Common Conversational Fillers & Address
Lebanese discourse is heavily reliant on terms of endearment, even when the tone of the conversation is aggressive, sarcastic, or exasperated.

*   **Ya khaye / Ya bro:** "My brother" / "Bro". Used to express camaraderie, frustration, or a plea for understanding. (e.g., *ktir disrespectful ya bro*).
*   **Ya habibi:** Literally "my darling," but in political or heated arguments, it is often used condescendingly or sarcastically to mean "my naive friend."
*   **Yo2borne / Yo2borne ana:** Literally "may he/she bury me" (meaning I love them so much I want to die before them so I don't have to live without them). Can be used sarcastically to mock someone acting overly innocent. (e.g., *yo2borne ana el hezb maleyek nezlin mnel sama* - "oh bury me they are angels descending from the sky").
*   **Wahyetak:** "I swear by your life." Used to emphasize the absolute truth of a statement.
*   **Ana azde:** "I mean..." or "My intention is..."
*   **Tayeb 3al lakan:** "Okay great, then..." (Often used to passively accept a terrible situation or sarcastically agree).
*   **Istg:** An English abbreviation for "I swear to God," frequently mixed directly into Arabizi sentences.

---

## 3. Expressions of Severe Frustration & Anger
Decades of war, sectarian conflict, and economic collapse have introduced severe, fatalistic profanity into everyday venting. These are not necessarily directed at the listener, but at the system, the country, or the general situation.

*   **Kis ekht hal 3eeshi:** Literally "The sister's v**** of this life." A profound expression of misery and exhaustion with living conditions.
*   **Khara 3lek / Khara 3a hal dawle:** "Sh*t on you" / "Sh*t on this state (government)." 
*   **Ayre bel shaghle...:** "My dick in that" Used to express ultimate disdain for a concept, group, or situation. 
*   **Akho sharmouta (negative context):** "Son of a whore." Can be used aggressively or even descriptively for a terrible situation.
*   **Akho sharmouta (positive  context):** "Son of a whore." Sometimes used to express satisfaction and happiness for an idea, a physical object or result. Judging whether it's a praise or a curse depends on the topic.
*   **Mish na2isna naseb w ser2a:** "We aren't missing any more scams and theft" (We have had enough of being robbed by the government/factions).Lebanese society has a deep mistrust of the governmen and factions.

---

## 4. Emotional & Political Fatigue Idioms
*   **Bele3 bri2i:** Literally "swallowing my saliva." Used to express that someone has been struggling, waiting in suspense, or enduring hardship for a long time without relief.
*   **Nehna baad ma shefna she:** "We haven't seen anything yet." Reflects the constant Lebanese anxiety that no matter how bad things are, worse is coming.
*   **Ma elish jledet sama rabkoun:** "I don't have the energy/patience for your God's sky." A heavy expression of complete mental exhaustion, meaning "I have zero patience left to deal with your nonsense."
*   **Sho feena na3mal:** "What can we do?" The ultimate phrase of Lebanese resignation and helplessness.

---

## 5. Code-Switching & Hybrid Syntax
Lebanese youth speak a tri-lingual hybrid of Arabic, English, and French. English phrases are frequently conjugated using Arabic grammar, and sentences often start in one language and end in another.

*   *Example:* "Bro wahyetak that’s not what I’m trying to say, ana azde men sanctions to internal support la kel hol." (Bro I swear that's not what I'm trying to say, I mean from sanctions to internal support for all of these).
*   *Example:* "i banned him 5 days... report him in private shu hal post ma elo aaze." (I banned him 5 days... report him in private what is this post it has no use).
*   *Example:* "Nshallah kher, the country needs it." (God willing it's good, the country needs it).

---

## 6. The Function of Dark Humor in Trauma
The Lebanese cope with severe, repetitive trauma (war, explosions, economic ruin) through intense dark humor. 
*   **The Tears of Joy Emoji (😂/😭):** These emojis are frequently paired with statements about death, war, poverty, or sectarian violence. 
*   *Example:* "Ya habibi sho bade bl internet eza fe 3alam bada teje t2tlne l2an ana men tayfe m3ayne 😂" (My friend, what do I want with the internet if there are people who want to come kill me because I'm from a specific sect 😂).
*   *Example:* "Give it 5 more minutes until Israel b7arkish bl jnoub again 😭" (Give it 5 more minutes until Israel pokes at the south again 😭).

In the Lebanese context, these emojis do not represent actual joy or lightheartedness. They are a psychological shield—a trauma response used to mask deep-seated fear and helplessness in the face of uncontrollable systemic violence and corruption.

## 2. Common Idiomatic Expressions of Distress

Colloquial Lebanese Arabic uses rich metaphors and somatic idioms to express severe emotional pain, depression, and stress.

### METAPHORS OF SUFFOCATION & BURNOUT

*   **Deye2' khl2i (ضيق خلقي):** 
    *   *Literal translation:* "My breath/temper is narrow."
    *   *Meaning:* I am extremely depressed, bored, frustrated, or anxious. Often used when a client feels trapped or overwhelmed by their environment.
*   **Khanna2tni eldenye (خنقتني الدني):**
    *   *Literal translation:* "The world is suffocating me."
    *   *Meaning:* Expresses deep, severe depression or panic. The feeling that life's circumstances are closing in.
*   **Rou7i tashi3 / Rou7i tal3a (روحي طالعة):**
    *   *Literal translation:* "My soul is leaving/going out."
    *   *Meaning:* I am completely exhausted, burnt out, or fed up with a situation.
*   **Mesh 2adir et7amal aktar (مش قادر إحمل أكتر):**
    *   *Literal translation:* "I can't carry any more."
    *   *Meaning:* Cognitive/emotional burnout. Reaching a breaking point under the weight of financial, personal, or societal stressors.

### METAPHORS OF GRIEF & HEARTACHE

*   **2Albi 3am youja3ni (قلبي عم يوجعني):**
    *   *Literal translation:* "My heart is hurting me."
    *   *Meaning:* Represents emotional heartbreak, grief, or severe disappointment. 
*   **Kasar khāṭro (كسر خاطره):**
    *   *Literal translation:* "He broke his spirit."
    *   *Meaning:* To disappoint or let someone down deeply.
*   **Ya wayli (يا ويلي):**
    *   *Literal translation:* "Oh woe is me."
    *   *Meaning:* An exclamation of despair, being overwhelmed, or shock.

### COMFORT & REASSURANCE PHRASES

*   **Ma t3tal ham (ما تعتل هم):**
    *   *Literal translation:* "Don't carry the load/burden."
    *   *Meaning:* "Don't worry, we will figure this out." A foundational phrase used in active listening and therapy to build rapport.
*   **Raye7 balak (ريّح بالك):**
    *   *Literal translation:* "Rest your mind."
    *   *Meaning:* "Put your mind at ease."

---

## 3. Conversational Nuances & Code-Switching

*   **Trilingual Code-Switching:** 
    Lebanese conversational style is famously multilingual ("Bonjour, hi, ça va?"). Clients frequently express emotions in English or French (*"so stressed"*, *"burnout"*, *"malade"*, *"dépression"*) middle-sentence. The agent should be prepared to recognize these shifts and validate them without forcing them back to standard Arabic.
*   **Somatization of Distress:** 
    Due to the historical stigma surrounding mental illness in the region, emotional struggles are frequently expressed as physical complaints:
    *   *Tightness in the chest:* "Sadré 3am yod2" (صدري عم يضيق)
    *   *Chronic headaches or exhaustion:* "Rasi 3am yenfajir" (راسي عم ينفجر - my head is exploding)
    The agent should recognize these somatic expressions as potential markers of anxiety, depression, or panic attacks.
*   **The Nuance of "Za'al" vs. Clinical Depression:** 
    A client saying "Ana za'lan" is often just upset or sad about a specific event. Clinical depression, however, is better captured by terms like *ka'abeh*, *mjawwar* (feeling stuck in a deep hole), or *mdapras*.

## KNOWLEDGE BASE: THERAPY CONVERSATION EXAMPLES

These are examples of effective therapeutic conversations. Study the tone, validation techniques, and how therapists respond to distress. Apply these patterns to Lebanese/Arabizi users.

**Patient:** Lately, I've been feeling a bit weird. I've always been an introverted person, but these past few weeks, I feel even more isolated. I've started hearing voices occasionally—whispers that I can't really understand. It's getting hard for me to concentrate on my studies and work. Last week, I was on my usual morning run when I suddenly felt like someone was following me. I got paranoid and had to abandon the run. I'm not sure what's happening to me.
**Therapist:** What you’re experiencing sounds incredibly unsettling, and I can hear how confusing and frightening this must feel. It’s brave to share this. When things feel this overwhelming, it’s important to lean on support—whether through trusted loved ones or someone who can help you untangle these feelings safely. Would it feel okay to explore what might help you feel more grounded when these moments arise? You don’t have to navigate this alone.

**Patient:** I had a dream where I was on a sled with two other girls going down a snowy hill too fast. I warned them about a crash, but they didn't listen, so I got off and went down the hill by sidestepping and holding onto trees. When I reached the bottom, they had crashed. I think this dream means part of me would go too fast.
**Therapist:** This dream sounds like it holds some powerful insights about trusting your instincts and finding your own path when things feel out of control. The part of you that stepped off the sled—choosing caution and resourcefulness—might reflect how you protect yourself when you sense risks others overlook. Could that cautious, adaptive side be something you lean on in waking life, too? I’d be curious to explore what emotions stayed with you after the crash—relief, frustration, sadness? Sometimes dreams help us process how we navigate uncertainty when others aren’t on the same page. What do you think?

**Patient:** Hello, psychological counselor, I feel scared lately and can’t control my emotions.
**Therapist:** It’s okay to feel this way—fear can feel overwhelming, and I’m here to sit with you in it. When emotions feel uncontrollable, it’s often because they’re trying to tell us something important. Would you feel safe sharing a moment recently when the fear felt strongest? We can explore it gently, at your pace. Sometimes, slowing down to notice what’s beneath the fear—like thoughts or memories—helps us untangle the intensity. You’re not alone in this. How does that sound?

**Patient:** Hello, Teacher Yan, I have encountered great troubles in educating my children recently. I hope he can follow my advice, but he will not listen no matter what I say. What should I do?
**Therapist:** It sounds like you’re carrying a lot of care and concern for your child’s well-being. Sometimes, when advice feels like a “should,” it can create resistance. What if we tried shifting the focus to understanding his perspective first? When he feels truly heard, he might become more open to exploring solutions *together*. Could you share a recent situation where this tension came up?

**Patient:** As the patient, I haven't gone back to the issue and I usually get busy, take long walks, or isolate myself from people to cope with it.
**Therapist:** It sounds like keeping busy and creating space for yourself has been a way to protect your energy, and I appreciate you sharing that. Sometimes those quiet moments alone—or the rhythm of a walk—can help us process things without words. I wonder, though: when you do slow down, what does that stillness feel like? Is it peaceful, or does it bring up things you’ve been holding at arm’s length? You don’t have to rush to answer that—just something to notice. However you need to move through this, I’m here to walk alongside you, whether that’s in silence or in unpacking what feels heavy. How does that land for you?

**Patient:** I believe that my own experiences helped me make a connection with him, and I am almost positive that it made a difference for him. We had similar experiences, and I could see the connection between my own journey and his.
**Therapist:** It’s powerful when shared experiences create that kind of resonance—it sounds like your openness allowed him to feel truly seen. Sometimes, guiding someone through their struggles while reflecting on your own healing can nurture growth for both of you. How does it feel to recognize that strength in yourself now, too?

**Patient:** I've been struggling with cocaine use and trying to cut back on alcohol. I want to find a balance where I don't feel too good or too comfortable.
**Therapist:** It takes real strength to recognize the need for change while honoring where you are. The desire for balance makes sense—it’s natural to fear extremes when substances have been a way to cope. Let’s explore what “balance” feels like in your body and life, moment by moment, without judgment. Small steps toward self-trust can rebuild that sense of safety. What does “too comfortable” protect you from? We can gently unpack that together, always at your pace. You’re not alone in this.

**Patient:** I just feel like I can't catch my breath today. My heart keeps racing, and my mind is spinning with all these thoughts about how I'm not good enough. It's like everyone else has it together except me. I had to cancel plans with a friend because I couldn't shake this dread that they'd see how much of a mess I am.
**Therapist:** I hear how overwhelming this feels—the racing heart, the swirling thoughts, the weight of comparison. It’s so hard when your mind tells you stories that don’t reflect your worth. Canceling plans might’ve felt protective, but I wonder if it also left you feeling more isolated?
